The problem

The chart records the decisions. The conversation is where the care happens — and today it vanishes.

Quality, training, and trust all depend on what was actually said: the question that wasn't asked, the dose that was mumbled, the moment a patient said "yeah, but…" and stopped being honest.

Manual review can sample maybe one visit in a hundred. OverChart listens to all of them — and shows who spoke, when, and over whom.

What it does

Hears the whole room

One small microphone per exam room captures every conversation, including interruptions — because clinical signal hides in the places where talk overlaps.

Knows who spoke

Not a wall of text. Who said what, when they said it, and to whom, reconstructed after the fact and timestamped to the second.

Understands clinical speech

Medication names, doses, and symptoms come through clearly in testing — no hand-tuned vocabulary, nothing to maintain drug by drug.

Reads like a colleague

The transcript is the product: something a supervisor can review, a teacher can learn from, and — later — a review assistant can score against your own standards. It advises no treatment; clinicians decide.
Why it's different

Made for real rooms.

Quietly small

No racks, no server room per clinic. OverChart lives on inexpensive, off-the-shelf hardware — from a device the size of a palm up to a central machine that serves a region.

Works through bad days

Recordings are captured locally and survive network outages, then sync when convenient. A slow afternoon is never a lost afternoon.

Conversations stay yours

Designed local-first: capture, processing, and storage sit wherever your policy says they must, with a small, auditable data path in and out.

One system, any shape

Lightweight recorders feeding central processing, or complete room systems that send only text — the same software either way, chosen per room.

Where it stands

Works today

  • The full pipeline — capture to reviewable transcript, running unattended, guarding its own memory and reporting its own failures.
  • A measuring culture — every change is tested against known-answer conversations, so "better" means provably better.
  • Hardware freedom — proven on advanced machines, designed down to commodity boxes.

Next — deliberately

  • No patient conversations have been processed. How real conversations are handled — privacy, retention, access — gets designed before any real recording exists.
  • A review assistant, not an oracle. Scoring visits against a rubric is the next layer; it will grade conversations, never give treatment advice.
  • Demo units are being assembled now — the goal is to place a working system on your desk, not a slide deck in front of one.
